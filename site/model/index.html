The models

How each forecast on this site is made: what goes in, what the model does with it, what comes out, and how it was tested.

Every model follows the same order of work: it is designed, tested on past elections, and then frozen. A forecast is locked before its election and scored against the results afterwards.

House Used in 2026, locked

A forecast for every House district and for control of the chamber. Most districts are never polled, so each one starts from its partisanship and the national mood, and moves only part of the way toward its own polls when it has any.

From inputs to forecast

What the model does
What comes out
  • Generic-ballot averages
  • Presidential approval, the last House result, gas prices

1National environment

The mean of five generic-ballot averages, blended with an estimate from the fundamentals. In 2026 the polls got 56% of the weight.

Fundamentals estimated on elections from 1962 to 2022.

  • The district's lean (Cook PVI, on the lines in force)
  • Whether the incumbent is running

2A starting point for each district

The district's lean on its current lines, the national environment (a district moves about 0.8 points for each national point), and about 4.5 points for an incumbent.

Estimated on 3,752 contested races, 2006 to 2024.

  • Polls of the district

3District polls

District polls are few and often paid for by a party. A sponsored poll is shifted against its sponsor (by about 6 points in 2026) before the polls are averaged.

The shift is estimated from past sponsored polls.

4Blend

The poll average and the starting point are weighed by how reliable each is. A polled district moves roughly half way to its polls; a district with none rests on its starting point.

  • How far polls and district forecasts missed in past elections

5Simulation

40,000 simulated elections, the same ones as the Senate: each draws one national polling miss for both chambers, then an error for each district. A seat with only one major party on the ballot is fixed.

What comes out
  • Each district: the margin, its range, and each side's chance of winning
  • The chamber: the spread of seat counts and the chance of a majority

Twice the state's lean, plus the national environment, plus 2 points for a sitting senator.

3Poll average

Recent polls count more (a half-life of 60 days), likely-voter polls count more than polls of registered voters or adults, and a party-sponsored poll is shifted against its sponsor.

5Simulation

40,000 simulated elections. Each draws a national polling miss shared by every race, a regional shock, a tilt by state lean and an error for each race, all with fat tails.

Governors Design frozen

A forecast for each governor race, and for how many each party wins. Governor races are polled less evenly than Senate races, so the model has an order of fallbacks: late polls, then earlier polls, then expert ratings.

From inputs to forecast

What goes in
What the model does
What comes out
  • Generic-ballot averages
  • Presidential approval, the last House result

1National environment

In an even year, the same blend of generic-ballot averages and fundamentals as the House and Senate. In an odd year there is no House election to poll, so it is estimated from approval and the last House vote, by the year of the presidential term.

  • The state's presidential lean
  • Whether the governor is running

2A starting point for each race

The state's presidential lean, averaged over two elections, plus the national environment, plus a term for an incumbent.

Fitted on even-year governor races, 1998 to 2024.

  • Polls of the race
  • Expert ratings, for a race without polls

3Polls, by one of three paths

A race with polls in its final 21 days uses their plain average. A race with none uses its polls from 22 to 60 days out. A race with no poll in 60 days adds the expert ratings to its starting point.

Polls from 22 to 60 days out miss by about 37% more than late ones.

4Blend

The poll average and the starting point are weighed by how reliable each is. A race with a strong third candidate gets a range one and a half times as wide.

  • How far polls missed in past governor races

5Simulation

20,000 simulated elections. A race shares the statewide polls' common miss in proportion to its weight on polls, and the national environment's error for the rest.

What comes out
  • Each race: the margin, its range, and each side's chance of winning
  • The count of races each party wins, with its range

    The candidates are known

    Sees The state's lean, the national environment, whether the governor is running, and the expert ratings.

    Does No poll counts yet. The forecast rests on the starting point and the ratings.

  2. 22 to 60 days out

    Earlier polls count

    Sees Polls of the race that ended in this window.

    Does They are used only while the race has no later poll, and they are trusted less than late polls.

  3. The final 21 days

    Late polls take over

    Sees Polls that ended in the final three weeks.

    Does Once a race has a late poll, its earlier polls drop out. The late polls are averaged with equal weights.

  4. Election day

    The forecast is locked

    Sees Nothing after the lock.

    Does The last run is frozen. Nothing is changed after that.

  5. Afterwards

    Scored against the results

    Sees The certified results.

    Does The locked forecast is scored once, and nothing is retuned after the score is read.

How it was tested

Even-year governor races from 2004 to 2024 were forecast again with every number fitted on earlier elections only. Each proposed change was written down before it ran and had to beat the design before it.

Electoral college In design

A presidential forecast for each state, added up to electoral votes. National polls set the popular vote, each state is placed against the nation, and the simulation decides how often the states fall together.

From inputs to forecast

What goes in
What the model does
What comes out
  • National polls of the two nominees

1National forecast

The margin between the two candidates in national polls, among all respondents, with recent polls counting more. Fundamentals were tested and are not used.

  • The last election's result in each state

2A starting point for each state

The national forecast plus the state's lean at the last election.

  • Polls of each state

3State polls

Each polled state's average is pulled part of the way toward its starting point: 10% to 29% of the way in the final month, and more earlier.

The pull is fitted on elections from 2000 to 2020.

  • Each state's share of the national vote

4The add-up

Every state moves by the same amount, so that the states, weighted by turnout, add up to the national forecast.

  • State demographics
  • How far state and national polls missed in past elections

5Errors that move together

One national miss shared by every state. States that are alike in their demographics miss together, there is a tilt by state lean, and each state has its own error on top.

  • Electoral votes for each contest

6The count

40,000 simulated elections over 56 contests: the states, the District of Columbia, and the districts of Maine and Nebraska. Each contest's winner takes its electoral votes.

What comes out
  • Each state: the margin, its range, and each side's chance of winning
  • The electoral vote count and the chance of reaching 270
  • The popular vote, and the chance it splits from the count
  • The state most likely to decide the election

Several of these pieces are adopted provisionally. They stand or fall when the model is scored on 2024, once, at the design freeze.

    Does The forecast replaces the board. A thinly polled state sits close to its starting point, and the ranges are at their widest.

  3. The months before

    Polls come in

    Sees New national and state polls. The conventions get no special treatment: three ways of handling them were tested and none passed.

    Does National polls move every state together; a state's own polls move it against the nation. The ranges narrow as the election nears.

  4. The final month

    States lean on their own polls

    Sees The late national and state polls.

    Does A polled state rests mostly on its own polls, with a small pull toward its starting point.

    PollsStarting point

    A polled state: 71% to 90% on its polls

  5. Election day

    The forecast is locked

    Sees Nothing after the lock.

    Does The last run is frozen. Nothing is changed after that.

  6. Afterwards

    Scored against the results

    Sees The count, as it finishes.

    Does The locked numbers are compared with the results. They are never revised.

In the backtest the forecast's centre was no closer a week out than 100 days out, and the early ranges were too wide. Both are open questions for the design freeze.

How it was tested

The elections of 2008 to 2020 were forecast again at 100, 60, 30 and 7 days out, with every number fitted on earlier elections only. The figures are a week out. The test checks for a plainly wrong model; it tested no change.

Where it stands

In design, with the freeze due by May 2028. A second model of a different kind is to be built before then, and both meet 2024 at the same time. There is no 2028 forecast until both parties have a presumptive nominee.

Primaries In design

Each candidate's chance of winning each state's primary or caucus, in both parties. Primaries are held in sequence, so the model follows the calendar: what a state's result says about the states that vote after it.

From inputs to forecast

What goes in
What the model does
What comes out
  • National primary polls
  • Who has announced, and who has withdrawn

1National average

Each candidate's share in national polls, with recent polls counting more: a half-life of 30 days before the first contest and 7 days once voting starts. A candidate's line ends when they withdraw.

The rules were fixed before any average was computed.

2A starting point for each state

Each candidate's national share, with undecided voters shared out in proportion.

  • The calendar: which states have voted, and which vote next
  • Results of the states that have voted
  • State demographics and region

3What earlier states say

How a candidate runs against their national standing is borrowed from states that have voted or been polled: an overall level, discounted for momentum, and a pattern carried to states of similar demographics and region.

  • Polls of the state

4The state's own polls

A state's own polls are blended in. The starting point counts for about half a poll, so a polled state follows its polls.

  • The open nomination races of 2008 to 2024

5Range

An error for each candidate's share, drawn many times to give the chance each candidate finishes first.

Six values are fitted, each with the race being forecast left out.

What comes out
  • Each state: every candidate's share and its range
  • Each candidate's chance of winning the state
  • Later: a delegate count and odds of the nomination

This election's own dataPast elections, used to set the model's numbers

The calendar and each party's delegate rules are inputs, so the model can be rerun when a date or a rule changes.

Through the campaign

  1. The year before

    Candidates announce

    Sees National and state polls of the declared candidates.

    Does Poll averages only. No chances are shown before the design is frozen.

  2. Before the first contest

    The early states are polled

    Sees Each candidate's national standing, the polls of each state, and the calendar as it stands.

    Does Each state is forecast from national standing and its own polls. A month before a state votes, this does no better than a plain poll average.

  3. Voting starts

    States vote in turn

    Sees Each result as it comes in, and the faster-moving national polls.

    Does A result shifts the forecast for later states, most of all for states like the ones that have voted. The gain over a plain poll average is in states without polls, once five or more states have voted.

  4. Any time

    A candidate withdraws

    Sees Announced withdrawals only.

    Does The candidate's line ends. Withdrawals that have not happened yet are not simulated.

  5. Each state's voting day

    Locked, then scored

    Sees Nothing after the lock.

    Does A state's last forecast before it votes is the one compared with its result.

How it was tested

The seven open nomination races from 2008 to 2024 were forecast state by state, 30 days and 7 days before each contest and on its day, each race on values fitted without it. The model was compared with a plain poll average.

Where it stands

In design. The first version, each candidate's chance in each state, is due by May 2027. A delegate count and odds of the nomination follow, published only once the field is down to three candidates.

Special elections Design settled, waiting for its first race

One House or Senate special election, forecast on its own date. There is no national seat count behind it: the model follows a single race from the vacancy to the result.

From inputs to forecast

What goes in
What the model does
What comes out
  • House: the district's Cook PVI, on the lines in force
  • Senate: the state's vote against the nation's in the last federal election

1The seat's lean

How far the district or state sits from the country as a whole.

  • Presidential approval
  • The last House vote
  • Recent House specials (House races)

2National environment on the date

There is no election-day generic ballot to lean on, so the environment is estimated from approval and the last House vote, by how much the date resembles a midterm. A House special adds the average swing in the specials of the past 12 months.

Estimated on elections from 1946 to 2022.

  • Senate: whether a sitting senator runs

3A starting point for the race

House: the House district model, for an open seat. Senate: lean plus environment, plus 2 points for a sitting senator who runs, an appointee included.

  • Polls of the race, entered by hand

4Polls, if there are any

A plain average of the polls that ended in the final 21 days or, with none there, 22 to 60 days out. It is weighed against the starting point by how reliable each is.

The poll rule passed its test on 31 polled House specials.

  • How far past specials missed

5Range

House: an error as wide as earlier specials' own forecast misses. Senate: a fat-tailed error with a standard deviation of 18 points.

What comes out
  • The margin
  • Its 50%, 80% and 95% ranges
  • Each side's chance of winning
